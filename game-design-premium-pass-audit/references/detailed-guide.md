# Premium pass audit

Adapted from Stanislav Stankovic's premium-pass audit. Keep its seven useful areas: value, progression velocity, free track, reward pacing, engagement, economy effects and recommendations. The fork removes universal value/return thresholds and unsupported judgments about designer intent.

## Value and alternatives

Identify price, season dates, premium versus free rewards, tier requirements, entry dates and competing offers. Separate earned currency from cash purchases, retail replacement cost from willingness to pay, and total rewards from rewards a player can attain.

Currency return is an accounting measure: 600 units returned against a 1,000-unit entry price is 60%. It does not determine whether buying is worthwhile. If useful, calculate retail-equivalent multiplier R/P and discount 100*(1-P/R), stating the valuation basis and excluding unpriced rewards rather than inventing precision. Use relevant marginal currency purchase rates, including bundle restrictions. Only compute ratios with positive denominators.

Do not label a retail-equivalent tier crossing as the point where a player has received their money's worth. A player may value a subset above the price or all rewards below it. Report the reward curve and attainable content directly.

Evaluate permanent exclusivity, early access and later availability as alternative policies. Examine willingness to pay, catalog access, substitution and lifecycle revenue. Permanent exclusivity is not a necessary condition for value.

## Progression velocity

Map XP from sessions, missions and events, including caps and missed opportunities. Estimate sessions and days to reach relevant rewards by purchaser segment and season entry date. State the intended completion distribution; do not impose a universal playtime fraction or require every buyer to finish. Test whether incomplete as well as complete participation offers value to the relevant audience.

Compare skip prices to marginal time saved and segment demand. Distinguish entry revenue from optional spend depth. The ratio of total optional skips to entry price does not identify designer intent.

## Free track and pacing

Identify what free players receive, what the free track teaches or promotes, and the counterfactual without it. Measure conversion and retention rather than assigning goodwill or resentment from structure alone.

Plot reward locations against cumulative time and value for relevant players. Compare front-loaded, back-loaded and even arrangements. Inspect long low-value stretches in context; five tiers is not a universal failure threshold. Back-loaded rewards can motivate completion and can also be irrelevant to late entrants. Estimate rather than assume these effects.

## Engagement

Separate sessions created from sessions rewarded that would otherwise occur. Examine mission constraints, reminders, deadlines and catch-up options. Changes can alter opportunity cost, salience or expected rewards; select a mechanism and test it. Do not infer guilt, coercion, addiction or long-term churn merely from obligations, deadlines or incomplete rewards.

## Economy

Model expected grants and sinks by segment and season, inventory carryover, progression acceleration and content exhaustion. Compare additional pass receipts with displaced shop purchases and renewal effects. A resource grant may change purchasing without causing a general price-level increase. Analyze the relevant marginal resource and constraint.

## Recommendation

State the observed issue or explicit hypothesis, affected players, mechanism, alternative, cost and falsification criterion. A short answer may cover only the user's decision. Synthetic challenge: a $12 pass with a cosmetic a player values at $15 and attainable currency worth $2 can be valuable despite low currency returns; do not generalize that one player's valuation to everyone.
