# Progression and monetization

## Progression as a wage

Treat earned progression per unit of engagement as a useful wage analogy. Ask what activity receives the reward, which players can earn it, what its marginal value is, and what remains to unlock. Progression can unfold systems or choices rather than simply raise a numerical level.

Raising the wage may increase engagement, accelerate content exhaustion, shift play between activities or reduce paid demand. It has no universal response curve. Compare free and paid progression on the same time and outcome basis. Evaluate diminishing rewards versus hard caps by their effects on the intended segments and lifecycle.

Reward placement matters as much as total reward quantity. Moving rewards from routine play to an event changes the activity being subsidized. Check eligibility, reach, timing and realized rewards before interpreting the intervention as an equal-value transfer.

## Passes, prices and spend depth

Analyze the entry price, optional purchases, reward access, season cadence, time requirements and alternatives. The value of a bundle includes the items players want and can actually obtain, not just currency refunded or a sum of shop list prices. Keep player value separate from developer economics and name any normative criterion being used.

Average daily monetization cap (ADMC) can compare spend ceilings: `(entry fee + total permitted optional tier spending) / available days`. Include the real purchase limits and pass availability. It is not ARPDAU, demand, expected spend or proof that raising the cap raises revenue.

An optional tier price buys time forgone. If tier effort rises while the skip price stays flat, the implicit money price of time falls at later tiers. Evaluate marginal XP pricing, bundles, stored skips or other alternatives against demand and player comprehension. None is automatically optimal.

For late-season entrants, compare remaining achievable rewards and time with those of early entrants. Discounts, gifted progress, catch-up rates and persistent access are alternatives to evaluate. Permanent exclusivity is one possible value component, not a prerequisite for a valuable pass.

Synthetic example: a $12 pass returns $2 of currency but gives immediate access to a cosmetic a relevant player values at $15. The currency-return ratio alone cannot establish whether that player receives value. Another player may value the same cosmetic at zero; do not generalize the first player's surplus to everyone.

## Content supply and marginal cost

Include production cost and cadence in the economic recommendation. Content architecture affects how cheaply a team can add meaningful variety. A cheaper production pipeline may change the feasible quantity/quality mix; a claim that quantity or quality always wins is not an economic argument.

Estimate incremental lifecycle value and cost, including effects on other offers or activities. Measure the return to the marginal content unit where possible. Maximum content volume, maximum engagement and maximum revenue are different objectives; identify the one the decision is meant to improve.

## Segments and competition

Use distributions, not just a representative spender. Distinguish player revenue concentration from day-to-day revenue volatility. State whether the denominator includes all users, active users or payers.

A leaderboard can target a broad population or an elite niche, and can have indirect aspiration or spectator effects. Assess its intended role, rank gradients, contest entry, effort and spending incentives, and spillovers. Neither elite targeting nor universal participation rewards establish success or failure on their own.
